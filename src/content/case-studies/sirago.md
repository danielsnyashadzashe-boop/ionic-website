## The engagement

Sirago is an underwriting manager operating within the Old Mutual Group. Like
most insurers of its size it had a transformation programme running, and like
most transformation programmes it was moving one process at a time.

The arithmetic was the problem. Each process needed months of analysis before
it reached a specification anyone could build from, and the programme as a
whole had run past eight months. That is not a sign of a badly run programme.
It is what thorough process work costs when it is done the traditional way,
through workshops, interviews, documents and a series of translations between
the people who know the work and the people who will change it.

There was a second cost, less visible on a plan and more expensive in
practice. Sirago's systems are built and maintained by Genric, another company
in the group. Requirements had to travel from Sirago's business teams to
Genric's delivery team, and meaning was lost on the way. Not through anybody's
fault; a requirement written by someone who does the work, read by someone who
will build it, is a translation, and translations lose things. What arrives is
a document that is technically complete and quietly missing the reason a step
exists.

## Why this engagement matters more than the numbers

This is the engagement that demonstrates what Process Genesis was built to do,
which is why it leads this portfolio.

The platform was not sold to Sirago as a tool for us to use on their behalf.
It was rolled out **across Sirago, for Sirago's own people to use**. The
underwriters, the operations staff and the team leads who actually know how
the work runs now capture, map and specify their own processes. Nobody had to
learn modelling notation. Nobody had to write a specification document. They
describe the work and review what comes back.

That distinction is the whole argument. A consultancy that maps your processes
leaves with the understanding. A business that maps its own processes keeps
it.

## What changed on the delivery side

Genric adopted the Process Genesis development module and now builds directly
from the requirements Sirago's people produce.

This is the part that removes the translation step rather than merely speeding
it up. Business and IT are no longer working from two documents that describe
the same process differently. They are working from the same maps and the same
requirements, with each requirement traceable back to the process step it came
from. When a developer asks why a rule exists, the answer is attached to the
rule.

It also means Genric is running Process Genesis as its own delivery tooling,
not as something imposed by a consultant for the duration of an engagement.
The platform stayed after we did.

## The outcome

Work that had taken eight months now takes under one, per process.

Two things are worth separating out of that figure, because the headline
number on its own invites the wrong conclusion.

**The time saved is analysis time, not build time.** Process Genesis compresses
the discovery, mapping, improvement and specification work that happens before
anyone writes code. It does not make the building faster. It makes the building
start sooner and start from something correct.

**The capability transferred.** The eight-month figure described what it cost
Sirago to have the work done. The one-month figure describes what it costs
Sirago to do the work. Those are different kinds of saving, and the second one
compounds every time the business touches another process.

## Why it belongs in this portfolio

Most transformation programmes fail at the same seam: the gap between the
people who understand the work and the people who change the systems. Sirago
and Genric sit on opposite sides of that seam, in different companies within
the same group, which is about as sharp a version of the problem as it gets.

Closing it there, with the business holding the pen, is the clearest evidence
we have that the approach works.
