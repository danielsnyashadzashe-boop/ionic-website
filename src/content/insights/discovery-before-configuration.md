There is a strong commercial incentive to start configuring quickly. Discovery
produces a document; configuration produces a screen someone can react to. When
a sponsor is waiting for evidence that money is turning into software, the
screen wins.

It is still the wrong order, and the reason is not methodological purity.

## Process documentation describes intent, not behaviour

Every organisation of size has process documentation. It is usually accurate
about what should happen and quietly silent about what does. The gap is not
negligence. It is what accumulates when a process meets four years of
exceptions, staff changes and systems that were replaced without the workflow
being revisited.

Configure against the documentation and you automate the intended process. The
real one continues alongside it, now with an additional system to work around.

We have yet to run a discovery exercise where the mapped reality matched the
documented process. That is not a criticism of any client. It is simply what
happens to processes over time.

## Discovery is what makes the business case falsifiable

The more valuable output of discovery is not the map. It is the number.

Discovery quantifies where cycle time is actually lost. That produces a specific
claim (this step consumes this much time, automating it is worth this much)
that can be checked afterwards. A business case built without it rests on a
percentage borrowed from a vendor's other customers.

This matters for a reason that is easy to miss: it changes what happens when the
programme underdelivers. With a quantified baseline you can identify which
assumption was wrong. Without one, the conversation becomes a dispute about
whether the programme worked at all, which nobody can win.

## The order also determines what you do not build

Discovery consistently finds steps that should be eliminated rather than
automated. Approval gates added for a control that no longer applies.
Reconciliations between two systems where one is now authoritative. Reports
produced monthly and read by nobody.

Automating these is worse than leaving them alone, because automation makes them
cheap enough to survive indefinitely. A manual step that wastes an hour a week
eventually gets questioned. An automated one never does.

## In practice

The Old Mutual engagement began as a pilot rather than a programme, with
discovery preceding configuration and expansion contingent on the result. It
became a five-year agreement across all business units.

We do not think the pilot succeeded because discovery is a superior methodology.
It succeeded because the pilot was scoped so that being wrong was survivable,
and discovery is what tells you, early and cheaply, which parts you were wrong
about.
