## The problem is arithmetic, not technology

Fewer customers carry coins every year. A car guard who depends on cash tips
therefore earns less each year through no fault of their own, while a customer
who genuinely wants to tip often has nothing in their pocket to do it with.

There is no behaviour to change on either side. The willingness already exists
and the money simply cannot move.

## One scan, and the guard watches it arrive

Every registered guard wears a lanyard with a QR card. The customer scans it
with their phone camera, sees the guard's name and photo so they know exactly
who they are paying, chooses an amount and pays. No app, no account, no
registration. For a payment aimed at a stranger in a car park, anything more
than that is a flow nobody finishes.

The guard sees the balance move on their own phone. That immediacy does more
work than it sounds like it should: nobody is waiting until the end of the week
to find out whether anything came in.

## Built for the person holding the phone

The app signs in with a phone number and a six digit code rather than an email
address and a password, because the login has to work for someone who does not
carry an email account around and cannot be locked out by a forgotten
credential.

## Two details that only matter once real people use it

**Duplicate identity checking** screens every registration, flagging exact
matches and near matches up to three digits apart with the differing digits
highlighted. It catches fraud, and much more often it catches typing errors,
which are commoner and just as damaging.

**Transfers are atomic and paired.** Moving a balance writes a matched debit and
credit under a shared reference and either completes on both sides or not at
all. There is no state where money exists in one place and not the other.

## Software was half the job

The other half is field work, and it is the half that decides whether a site
succeeds. Every rollout means loading each guard onto the system, generating and
linking QR codes, producing and issuing lanyards, installing the app on each
guard's own phone one at a time, and training them individually and on the
floor. Then staying: repeat sessions, watching whether tips are actually
flowing, and handling the practical failures that decide adoption.

Tippa takes a commission on tips. That is how the platform funds itself, and it
is the one honest disadvantage against a personal eWallet, which is free.
